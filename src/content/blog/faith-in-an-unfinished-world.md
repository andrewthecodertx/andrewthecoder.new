This is a continuation of
["God and Contingency"](https://andrewthecoder.com/blog/god-and-contingency),
["Process, Time, and the Self"](https://andrewthecoder.com/blog/process-time-and-the-self),
and
["The End of Self"](https://andrewthecoder.com/blog/the-end-of-self).
The earlier posts argued that a contingent world calls for a bipolar God,
that divine power is persuasive rather than coercive, and that providence
is the ceaseless offering of fresh possibility rather than the execution of
a fixed plan. This one asks a different question: if that is what God is
like, how do you actually live, pray, and hope in such a world?[^1]

Process theology is often introduced as a set of metaphysical claims about
God and the world, and it is that. But from the beginning it has also been
a proposal about spirituality: how to inhabit an unfinished universe with a
God who is not the all-controlling architect but the "fellow sufferer who
understands" and the "poet of the world."[^2] What follows is my attempt to
describe what that looks like from the inside.

### From Metaphysics to Practice

Process theology begins with a simple conviction that turns out to be
surprisingly disruptive: reality is made of events and relations rather
than static substances.[^3] Everything that exists, from electrons to human
lives, is caught up in a web of becoming in which each new moment inherits
a past, entertains possibilities, and then actualizes one of them.
Whitehead called these building blocks "actual occasions" and described the
universe as a "creative advance into novelty."[^4]

When process thinkers apply this to God, they do not conclude that God is
just another process among others. Instead they argue that God is the one
who gives coherence and direction to the whole advance, the source of
possibilities and the one who receives every actuality into the divine
life.[^6] Hartshorne spoke of a "neoclassical" theism in which God combines
an abstract, unchanging pole with a concrete, changing pole, and is
therefore both supremely stable and supremely responsive.[^5] Process
theology, in this sense, is simply classical theism updated for a world
that actually changes.

If that were all, it would remain a purely conceptual proposal, interesting
for metaphysicians and perhaps for those who like to tidy up philosophical
puzzles. But the moment you take seriously the claim that God is affected
by what happens, something more existential comes into view. In a process
frame God is not just the explanation of why anything exists at all; God is
the one whose life is partly constituted by what we become, and who then
works with what we have become to open up new possibilities for what might
yet be.

That shift has consequences for how you think about trust, prayer, hope,
and responsibility.[^7] The rest of this essay is an attempt to trace those
consequences, at least far enough to show why I still find this God worth
believing in and living before.

### Trusting a Relational God

Classical theism often grounds trust in God's control. God is the immutable
source of being, the one whose will is the hidden script behind history,
the sovereign who permits what happens and could have prevented any of
it.[^8] If that is how you think of God, then trusting God means believing
that, somehow, everything that happens fits into a plan that God could have
chosen otherwise.

Process theology cuts that knot. It insists that a world with genuine
contingency and creaturely freedom cannot be the execution of a finished
blueprint hidden in the divine mind.[^9] It follows that God does not stand
over against history as an all-determining cause, but is in history as its
most faithful participant, the one who lures each new moment toward the
best possibility actually available to it and then receives whatever we do
with that lure into the divine life.[^10]

On this view, trust cannot mean confidence that God secretly controls every
outcome. Instead it means confidence in God's character and constancy: that
God will always offer the best possibilities compatible with the world as
it really is, that God will never abandon what the world has become, and
that nothing we are or suffer is ever discarded or forgotten in God. Trust
becomes less about believing that everything happens for a reason, and more
about believing that nothing, however senseless, is beyond God's capacity
to take up into some future work of healing and transformation.

This is not a sentimental adjustment. It costs something to give up the
idea that God could have simply prevented a particular tragedy. It also
changes the emotional tone of faith. Instead of trying to make peace with a
hidden plan, you are invited to trust a God whose greatness lies in a
different register: not in unilateral power, but in inexhaustible
relational fidelity, in what Cobb and Griffin call "creative-responsive
love."[^11]

### Prayer as Cooperation

Nowhere does this relational picture matter more, or feel stranger, than in
prayer. If God does not possess coercive power to override the world's
causal structures at will, what exactly are we doing when we pray?

John Cobb has suggested that most traditional forms of prayer actually make
more sense from a process point of view than from classical philosophical
theism.[^12] The reason is simple: if every event in the world is connected,
and if God works in and through those connections rather than by
intermittent interventions from outside, then our praying is one more real
event that can enter into the web of causes and conditions God is already
using. Prayer, in that sense, is not a supernatural exception to the way
the world works; it is one way the world works.

Process thinkers are blunt about the negatives. Prayer is not magic, and it
does not suspend the ordinary patterns of causality.[^13] A world where God
could freely override those patterns at will, without abolishing creaturely
freedom and the regularities that make action possible, is not a coherent
world. But once that is cleared away, a surprisingly robust picture of
prayer remains.

On the process account, God is present to every occasion of experience,
offering an "initial aim" that reflects both the best possibilities
inherent in that situation and God's own purposes for richer, more just,
more loving forms of life.[^10] Our decisions do not create the aim, but
they do decide how fully it is received, distorted, or rejected. When I
pray, I am not informing God of facts God does not know, nor am I trying to
push God into doing what God is reluctant to do. I am, at minimum, opening
myself more attentively to the divine lure in the situation, which changes
how I act and, therefore, what God has to work with in the next moment.

Marjorie Suchocki puts it sharply: "God works with the world as it is in
order to bring it to where it can be."[^14] Prayer changes the "isness" of
the world simply by being one of the things that happens in it; a world in
which this specific praying occurs is not the same as a world in which it
does not. On a relational view of divine power, that difference matters to
God, because it alters the complex field of possibilities within which God
is already at work.

This reframes intercessory prayer in particular. To pray for another person
is not to route around their freedom or to ask God to suspend the medical,
psychological, or social conditions that shape their life. It is to offer a
concrete act of attention and desire that can enter into those conditions,
changing what is possible for them and for God. That may look like a shift
in my own behavior, a new capacity for compassion or persistence; it may
look like a subtle change in the emotional field of a relationship; it may
not look like anything I can see from the outside. But if the world is
truly relational, and if God works persuasively within those relations,
then prayer genuinely makes a difference, not because God was waiting for a
quota of requests to be met, but because our praying is one of the ways God
gains new avenues for gracious influence.

### Hope without Guarantees

The hardest questions arise when you ask what this looks like in the face
of suffering and death. Earlier in this series I argued that process
theology can confidently affirm what Whitehead called "objective
immortality": every experience is taken into the consequent nature of God,
where it is preserved and woven into the divine harmony.[^15] That already
changes the stakes of our lives. Nothing that happens is simply lost to the
void; everything remains eternally significant to God and can become
material for future possibilities.[^16]

But objective immortality is not the same thing as subjective immortality,
and process thinkers are divided on whether the latter can be affirmed.
Hartshorne was content with the former and skeptical about the latter;
Griffin has argued, cautiously, for the metaphysical possibility of a
continuing personal existence grounded in God's ongoing provision of
aims.[^17] What they share is a refusal to claim more than the metaphysics
will support.

For me, the question is existential rather than technical: is it enough to
know that my life will never be forgotten by God, if I cannot be certain
that I will be there to experience whatever redemption God brings from
it?[^18] Classical accounts of resurrection hold out the stronger claim
that the one who suffered is also the one who is made whole, and there is a
deep pull to that picture.[^19] Process eschatology, at least in its more
cautious forms, cannot simply take that for granted.

Here I find myself pulled in two directions at once. On the one hand, I do
not want to domesticate God by attributing to God coercive powers that
contradict the very relational picture that drew me to process thought in
the first place.[^20] If God cannot unilaterally determine the future
without abolishing the kind of world that makes freedom and love possible,
then God also cannot unilaterally guarantee a specific outcome for every
person, at least not in a way that bypasses the real risks built into
creation. On the other hand, I do not want to pretend that this leaves hope
untouched. It does not.

What remains is a humbler, and to my mind more honest, hope. I can hope,
with Griffin, that personal survival is metaphysically possible and that
the God whose purposes are always directed toward well-being will realize
such survival where it can be realized.[^21] I can trust that nothing in my
story, or anyone's story, is beyond God's capacity to take up into some
future good, whether that good is experienced by the person themselves or
by others who are touched by their life. I can refuse to claim certainty
where the best theological and philosophical work I know says that
certainty is out of reach.

Hope, in this register, is less an assurance that "it will all work out"
and more a stance toward God and the future: a decision to live as if love
is never wasted, as if God is always working for the greatest possible good
in every situation, and as if the meaning of our lives is held safe in a
reality larger than any single outcome we can imagine.

### Responsibility and Co-Creation

If God cannot simply fix things from outside, then responsibility shifts in
a very concrete way. We are no longer participants in a drama whose ending
has already been written; we are co-authors, with God and with one another,
of a story whose next chapter genuinely depends on what we do. That is
exhilarating on some days and heavy on others.

Process writers sometimes speak of "process spirituality" or "relational
spirituality" to name this style of life.[^7] It is less about achieving a
final product and more about learning to respond, moment by moment, to the
divine call toward deeper compassion, justice, and beauty. It asks not
"What has God already decreed?" but "What possibilities for good are
present here, and how can I cooperate with them?"

In practice, this means that the ordinary choices of a day matter more than
any abstract system. How I write code, how I speak to my children, how I
spend my money, how I respond to injustice in my community, all of these
become sites where God's persuasive power is at work and where my response
can either widen or narrow the possibilities available to others. There is
no realm of "mere" nature or "mere" secular activity where this is not the
case, because on a process view everything is relational, everything
participates in the ongoing creative advance, and everything touches God's
life in some degree.

This does not mean that the weight of the world rests on my shoulders.
God's faithfulness does not depend on my performance, and process thinkers
are clear that divine creativity is always at work, coaxing unexpected good
even from failures and refusals. But it does mean that I cannot hide behind
a belief that "if I do not act, God will take care of it anyway." In a
genuinely open world, some goods will only ever exist if someone,
somewhere, cooperates with the lure that makes them possible.[^22]

### A Way of Life in Process

If I had to compress all of this into a single picture of what it means to
live faithfully before the God of process theology, it might look something
like this.

You wake up in a world that is not finished, and you will go to sleep in a
world that still is not finished. God has not written the last page of
history in advance; God is writing it with us, responding with unexhausted
creativity to what we and the rest of creation have already become. Every
moment arrives with an invitation toward some richer, more just, more
truthful way of being, tailored to the concrete conditions of that
moment.[^10]

Living faithfully means learning to notice those invitations, to welcome
them more often than you ignore them, and to act on them in small and large
ways. It means praying not to escape the world or to bend God to your
will, but to become more available to God's work in the world and more open
to the costly possibilities that work entails. It means grieving honestly
when the world breaks in ways that cannot be mended within the limits of
this history, without rushing to explain those breaks as part of a hidden
plan.

Above all, it means trusting that, even without a guarantee of a neat
ending for every individual story, nothing is lost.[^15] The God who feels
the world feels you. The God who remembers the world remembers you. The
God who lures the world toward new forms of good includes every act of
love, every fragment of courage, every small refusal of cruelty, in that
work.

I do not pretend that this resolves all the questions the earlier essays
raised. It does not. What it does give, at least for me, is a way to stand
before God without having to choose between intellectual honesty and
religious trust, between acknowledging the real risks of an unfinished
universe and believing that my life, and the lives of those I love, matter
eternally to the one in whom that universe lives and moves and has its
being.
